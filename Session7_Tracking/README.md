# Tracking

## Introduction
When you collect time series, you are most likely interested in the movement of objects in space and time.  The objects could be cells on a dish, cells or nuclei in a developing animal or plant, subcellular compartments such as mitochondira, vesicles, etc.. within living cells, (single) molecules moving on a slide, etc..  Tracking these objects over time will give you information about their speed, direction, type of motion (is it a random walk, or is there directionality), and can also be used for lineage tracing of cells/nuclei within origanisms.

Tracking usually starts with segmentation of objects (using the approaches you have practiced in the previous sections), followed by assembly of tracks that represent the same object in each consecutive frame.  The higher the density of objects in the image, the harder it is to determine which object is which in the next frame.  You will want to use all information about the objects that is available (such as location, size, shape, intensity, etc..) to most reliably link objects from one frame to the next.  Thus, tracking is harder with objects that revela little (such as single molecules, which all look the same) and you should collect as much dictinguising data about your objects as possible.  The density of objects, and the ease with which they can be identified determine how far they can travle between frames and still be identified reliably.  You will want to have some idea about this when acquiring the data to determine the optimal frame rate for adata acquisition. 

An often encountered problem is the object disappearing from your segmented images for one or more frames.  You can either end a track when this happens, continue looking in later frames, or possibly even go back to the raw images to see if the object was missed during segmentation.  Obviously, all this will complicate your code, and you will only want to go that route when absolutely needed.
